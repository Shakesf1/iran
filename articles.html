How the US Plans to End the Iran War

Trump's 15-point framework, Iran's defiant response, and why the divide-and-conquer gambit is unlikely to succeed.

DW — US 15-Point Plan DW — Who Is Trump Talking To?

The Proposed Framework

Trump has been vocal about having won the war and that Iran is desperate for a deal. The US has reportedly conveyed a 15-point plan to Iran via Pakistan — which has offered to host any bilateral talks. A peace team has been assembled from the US side: JD Vance, Marco Rubio, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner. Islamabad, Pakistan is the intended location.

The reported elements of the plan include:

  • Nuclear disarmament — dismantle the full nuclear programme and hand over all enriched uranium
  • Limitations on ballistic and cruise missiles and other asymmetric capabilities
  • Iran to guarantee unconditional safe passage for all international shipping through the Strait of Hormuz
  • Ceasing support for proxy groups (Hezbollah, Hamas and others)
  • In return: sanctions lifted and assistance provided for civil nuclear energy

Trump also stated Iran is willing to hand a "very big gift" related to oil and gas. Witkoff and Kushner have proposed a one-month ceasefire during which the two sides would negotiate the full 15-point agreement.

Reported Israeli position

Israel's UN Ambassador Danny Danon stated Israel is not part of any reported talks. "As we speak, Israel and the US continue to target military targets in Iran, and we will continue to do that." It remains unclear how much Israel supports the plan — or whether it was consulted.

On Paper, Not a Bad Deal for Iran — Or So It Seems

In essence, what the US offers is to restore global energy stability with no real regime change, in exchange for Iran giving up any and all nuclear arms — now and in the future. At first glance this does not look like a catastrophically bad deal for Tehran.

However, Iran was still in active diplomatic talks about the nuclear programme when the US and Israel struck. They had a prior deal with the US under Obama — capping uranium enrichment — which Trump unilaterally tore up in 2018. No action by the US has since provided any foundation of trust that a deal remains a deal.

Giving up control over the Strait of Hormuz would strip Iran of its single most powerful strategic weapon. Dismantling its missile programme would eliminate its asymmetric warfare capability. Together, those two concessions would leave Iran entirely defenceless against the next round of pressure — or the next administration that decides a treaty is inconvenient.

Iran's Response: Contempt

"Don't call your failure an agreement."
"Have your internal conflicts reached the point where you are negotiating with yourself?"
"Our first and last word has been the same from day one, and it will stay that way: Someone like us will never come to terms with someone like you. Not now, not ever."

— Zolfaghari, spokesperson for the Iran Military, pre-recorded televised address

This followed Trump's abrupt reversal the day prior on peace talks — a move denied by multiple actors inside Iran. It remains unknown with whom the US is actually negotiating. Senior leaders are under direct threat of assassination after the wave of targeted killings by US and Israeli forces. It is unlikely those leaders would expose themselves to any negotiation channel.

The Probable US Gambit: Divide and Conquer

The most plausible US play is to pull a rabbit out of a hat: find an opportunistic Iranian figure who sees this as a chance to become the new leader. A second- or third-rate figure within the current regime who can be pushed forward, sign a deal, and hope that the Iranian population — many of whom want rid of the current leadership — will rally behind him. The US claims victory, secures access to energy resources, and gets out.

Potential Interlocutors

A Pakistani official named Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi and parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Qalibaf as contact persons. Qalibaf is viewed as the de-facto wartime leader following the killing of Ali Larijani. Previously an unsuccessful presidential candidate on three occasions, he is described as "deeply ambitious". Politico, citing two US officials, said Qalibaf "could lead Iran and negotiate with the Trump administration in the war's next phase." Qalibaf denies any contact with the US. Hassan Rouhani (president 2013–2021) and current President Masoud Pezeshkian are also seen as moderates who could favour a negotiated settlement.

This is not entirely dissimilar from Venezuela — ignore existing channels, create your own, and hope for the best while claiming oil resources. But it ignores the structural strength of the IRGC and its demonstrable grip on Iranian society. The US is banking on internal collapse after executing many senior leaders, hoping the IRGC is no longer holding a firm enough grip to resist. That is a very large bet.

Assessment

That is ultimately the problem of Trump's incompetence: he has destroyed US credibility as a negotiating counterparty. Giving up Hormuz removes Iran's strongest bargaining chip. Giving up missiles removes asymmetric deterrence. Doing both simultaneously — to a government that watched the US walk away from the last nuclear deal — is a capitulation that no Iranian leader could survive politically.

The new supreme leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has yet to make a public appearance; unconfirmed reports suggest he may have been killed or is severely injured. That power vacuum is precisely what the US is trying to exploit. Whether any credible figure steps into it and accepts a deal on these terms is, at this point, a very low-probability outcome.

Bottom line: The US peace framework is structured in a way that strips Iran of every meaningful deterrent in exchange for economic normalisation. Iran's public posture — and its historical experience with US treaty commitments — makes acceptance deeply unlikely under current leadership. The more probable near-term outcome is continued attrition while the US searches for a more pliable Iranian interlocutor.
