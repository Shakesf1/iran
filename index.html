Monitoring the escalation in the Iran War and who controls the Strait (as they win the war)

The Iran War was initiated by the US and Israel on the 28th of February. Initial military objectives remain opaque, however currently only one thing truly matters: Control of the Strait of Hormuz. If Iran remains in control over the Strait, the US will be deemed to have lost the war. Despite posturing and rhetoric to the contrary.
To monitor this we track the number of attacks from Iran (or its allies) - as their ability to strike also enables them to threaten the Strait and thereby influence global markets. Furthermore, we monitor actual shipping activity near the Strait as well as economic risk proxies such as the BDTI and oil spreads, which reflect the economic friction caused by the conflict. Casualties are also tracked, although with a lag of 24-48h due to the reporting lag.
Iran's continued control of the Strait will have impact on global inflation and thereby GDP growth. Every sustained 10% rise in oil prices reduces global GDP by 0.2% and adds 25 basis points to headline inflation.

The goal is simple: provide a transparent, near real-time data driven view on the conflict. It will help us tell the outcome of the war, and therefore impact on the market.

About, Methodology & Sources

Strategic Escalation Score

A multi-factor composite index measuring theater intensity. Values > above 1 signal active escalation; while values below -1 indicate significant cooling. The score integrates four pillars: Kinetics (launch acceleration), Maritime Friction (VLCC passing through Hormuz), Energy Risk (spread between Murban and Brent Crude) and Economic Cost of shipping (log changes in BDTI). This hybrid Z-score approach balances physical battlefield tempo with global market stress to provide a timely indicator of escalation or de-escalation. Most recent observation updates intraday as new data arrives (every 30 minutes)

Executive Summary: April 6, 2026 (Day 38 of the War)

Trump went full on crazy on 'Truth Social' over the last few days. Threatening Iranian civilian infrastructure and bombing Iran back to the stone age. His initial deadline was at April sixth but it, again, has been delayed. Now the deadline is April 7th.

Luckily, other countries have taken up the mantle of diplomacy. Pakistan had send an agreement for cease fire to both the US and Iran. As of now, both countries have rejected the terms. Iran because it wants a permanent solution, not a temporary one. They replied with a 10-clause response which include reparations. The US said the plans were not strict enough. As such, the threat of Trump hangs over the conflict (and markets). Although, markets are reacting to Trumps antics less and less. Except for oil, which has risen over the last few days but equity markets hardly moved.

Talking about markets: It seems to underprice the effect of continued closure of Hormuz. There will be physical shortages soon (which Twitter messages cannot resolve) and second order effects (e.g. agriculture) will also happen. Equity markets still haven't responded much to this soon-to-be reality.

Last but not least: Hormuz we see more and more ships passing through, although this is all negotiated with Iran. As such it is not an opening but a serious degradation compared to the pre-war situation which also becomes a structural negative/risk premium going forward.

The Murban-Brent spread reflects the geopolitical risk of the Gulf. Sharp changes can signal rising tanker risk and geopolitical stress as markets price in potential disruption around the Strait of Hormuz.

Maritime Economic Risk (BDTI)

Tracking the daily cost of crude oil shipping via the Baltic Dirty Tanker Index. This primarily tracks Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) which carry heavy crude oil. Therefore good proxy. Also reflects hidden costs such as insurance, fuel and security premia.

Signal Logic

The Escalation Score utilizes an Expanding Window with Exponential Decay (EWMA) applying a 0.97 decay factor on daily data (last day uses intraday update). Starting generally at beginning of the war, except for market metrics which start earlier to get a proper baseline.


Kinetic: Tracks strikes of Iran and its proxies. Initial baseline assumed at 0 prior to conflict.
Maritime: Monitors VLCC transits through Hormuz (Pre-war mean: 10).
Energy: Analyzes Murban-Brent spread as a localized risk premium indicator.
Shipping: Tracks BDTI log returns, incorporating insurance and freight volatility.


Methodology & Data Sources

Multi-Source OSINT: Aggregated real-time intelligence from Telegram channels, Israeli Home Office Command alerts, and regional news wires. It tracks theater spread across conflict zones.

Baltic Exchange Data: Reflects daily cost fluctuations in crude shipping (BDTI). This captures the market's "Risk Premium," including insurance and security surcharges.

Shipping AIS (VLCC Tracking): Proprietary scraping of satellite/terrestrial AIS networks. This monitors VLCC movements through the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint for global oil supply. It provides near real-time insights into maritime activity and potential disruptions.
